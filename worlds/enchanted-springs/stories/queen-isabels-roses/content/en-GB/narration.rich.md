# Queen Isabel's Roses

*Read aloud, or let an older child read it themselves. Pause often so everyone can
puzzle, wonder, and try ideas. There are no wrong answers here, only new paths to
follow.*

## Before you begin

In the old riverside city of Coimbra, with its climbing stone streets and its
castle on the hill, there lived a queen the people loved: good Queen Isabel. Her
delight was to carry bread to those who had none, and she did it in secret, slipping
out at dawn with a covered basket, because a kindness done for thanks, she said, is
only half a kindness.

On the morning this story begins, the Queen was unwell and could not rise. She
called you to her side and pressed her warm hands over yours. "I cannot make my
round today," she said, "and there are hungry mouths who will go without. Will you
carry the kindness in my place? Take bread to those who need it, and do it softly,
the way I would, asking nothing back."

You promised that you would, and went down toward the waking city, the castle bells
tolling gently behind you.

*What is your hero name? Which three magics will you carry: Bloom, Truth,
Starlight, or Riversong?*

## Stop 1: The Castle Kitchen

The castle kitchen was warm and golden, and its long table was heaped with rosca
bread, ring upon ring of it, far more than the castle could eat before it went
stale. By the hearth the old cook dozed, chin on chest, snoring softly.

Your task was a quiet one: to take only enough to share, no more than was fair,
and to carry it away without waking the cook or drawing any eye, so the giving
stayed as humble and secret as the Queen wished.

*How much do you take, and how do you carry it out without a sound? How do you keep
the kindness from being noticed? What is your plan?*

When your basket held just enough, covered over with a clean cloth, you stepped out
into the cool morning air without so much as a creak of the door. The cook slept
on, none the wiser.

## Stop 2: The Winding Streets

You carried the covered basket down into the maze of narrow streets, and there came
the harder question: of all these doors, who needed the bread most? The Queen never
gave at random; she gave where it mattered. So you looked closely, the way she
would have. A chimney with no smoke rising. A doorstep where a thin cat waited in
hope. A small, pale face at a window with no breakfast behind it.

*Who do you think is in need this morning, and how can you tell? And how will you
give the bread so that nobody is shamed by taking it?*

When you left your loaves quietly at the right doors, with a soft word and never a
fuss, the people who found them looked up in astonished gladness, and could not
think whom to thank. The cold streets seemed to warm behind you as you went.

## Stop 3: The Watchman's Gate

But to come and go from the castle you had to pass the gate, and at the gate stood
the watchman: broad, stern, and sharp-eyed, with orders to keep. He stepped into
your path and held up a hand. "Hold there. What is it you carry under that cloth?"
he demanded. "The King has said plainly that the castle's bread is not to be given
away at the gate, and I mean to see what you are about."

He was no villain, only a man doing his duty as he understood it, and he would not
simply wave you through. He wanted the basket opened.

*What do you say to the watchman? Will you tell him the truth of your errand? How
can you help him see that carrying bread to the hungry is a good and gentle thing,
worth his blessing rather than his blame?*

When you answered him honestly, without fear and without a lie, and spoke of the
Queen's kindness as the good thing it was, the hard set of his face began to ease.
"Very well," he said, gentler now, almost curious. "Lift the cloth, then, and let
me see for myself."

## Stop 4: The Bread Becomes Roses

You drew a steadying breath and lifted the cloth, ready to show him everything and
tell the whole truth, come what may. But under the cloth there was no bread at all.

Where the golden loaves had lain, the basket now brimmed with roses: fresh white and
blushing pink, heavy with dew and scent, tumbling over the rim, though it was far
from the season any rose should bloom. The kindness had become a small, bright
miracle in your hands.

*How does this make you feel, holding a basket of impossible roses? What do you say
to the watchman now? Could your Bloom magic help the roses open fuller and
brighter still?*

The watchman looked, and looked again, and the sternness simply fell from him. There
was nothing here to forbid, nothing to scold, only flowers out of season and a
secret too gentle to punish. The Queen's quiet giving was safe, hidden inside a
wonder, and the watchman, of all people, found he was smiling.

## Roses All the Way Home

As you made your way back through Coimbra, the roses would not stay in the basket.
They began to bloom along the tops of the walls, around the door frames, in the
cracks of the old stone steps, until the whole climbing city wore them like a
garland. People came out to wonder, and finding themselves glad, began to share
whatever they had with one another.

The watchman picked up an empty basket and asked, almost shyly, if he might help
carry bread come the next dawn. And high in her room good Queen Isabel heard the
laughter rising from the streets, and smiled to herself, for she knew that a
kindness done softly had filled an entire city with flowers.

You did it together. You were honest when it would have been easier to hide, and
you were kind when no one was watching, which is the kindest kind of all. Well done.
