# The Great Wooden Horse

*Read aloud, or let an older child read it themselves. Pause at each question so
the heroes can decide together. There are no wrong answers here, only braver ideas
to try.*

## Before you begin

For longer than anyone could properly remember, the two camps on the windswept
shores of Troy had been caught in a tired old quarrel. Down on the long beach the
seafarers lived among their tents and their wooden ships with painted eyes. Up on
the hill the proud city stood behind its great honey-coloured walls, with its
gates barred fast. Nobody could say how it had started, and the strange thing was
that nobody on either side really wanted it any more. Both camps were weary to the
bone, and both, in their secret hearts, longed for it simply to end.

Odysseus the Bright called the two of you to his side. He had a short curly beard
and a kind, twinkling smile, and he turned a tiny carved horse over in his hands
as he spoke. "I have had an idea," he said, "and it is a better one than any
shouting across the sand. Let us build a great wooden horse, as tall as a house.
But we shall not fill it with soldiers, as the old, sad stories might. We shall
fill it with gifts and music and a hand of friendship, and roll it up to the
gates as a happy surprise. A kind surprise can end a quarrel that a thousand cross
words never could."

Into your hands he pressed three gifts: a basket of honey and figs, a ball of
Penelope's thread, and a great spiral conch horn. "Remember," he said, "on these
shores we do not win by being the strongest. We win by being brave, loyal to a
friend, and full of ideas."

*What is your hero name? Which quality will you carry: Imagination, Courage, or
Loyalty?*

## Stop 1: Building the Horse

The builders had gathered great heaps of smooth wooden planks along the sand, but
heaps of wood are not yet a horse. It had to stand tall and strong on its wheels,
balanced so it would not wobble or topple, with a neat little door cut into one
side.

The carpenters scratched their heads and looked to you. How many planks would each
sturdy leg need? How should the curved pieces of the great barrel body be fitted
so the whole thing balanced? Penelope's thread was just the tool for measuring a
piece before it was cut, and careful counting would tell you exactly how many
planks the builders must bring.

*How could you build it so it stands straight and steady? Could you measure each
piece with the thread? Could you count the planks out together and work out the
balance? What is your plan?*

When the pieces were measured, counted, and fitted together just so, the great
wooden horse rose at last upon its wheels: tall, proud, and steady as the walls
themselves. The whole beach broke into a cheer.

## Stop 2: Filling It with Friendship

The horse was built, and its little side door stood open like a question. Now came
the heart of Odysseus's idea: what should go inside? Not soldiers, and not
anything frightening. The entire point was a gift, a surprise so kind that no shut
gate could stay shut against it.

You looked over all the lovely things laid out along the sand: the basket of ripe
figs and golden honey, garlands of bright flowers gathered from the hillside, and
a small drum for making happy music.

*Which things would make the kindest surprise? Which gifts whisper, "let us be
friends at last"? Choose them together. There is truly no wrong answer here, only
warmer ones.*

When you had tucked the figs, the flower garlands, and the little drum safely
inside, the great horse seemed to glow with welcome, like a present that can
hardly wait to be opened. It was, beyond any doubt, the friendliest horse that the
shores of Troy had ever seen.

## Stop 3: The Surprise at the Gates

Slowly, and with many willing hands, you rolled the great horse up the long hill
to the gates of the city. High on the honey-coloured walls the city folk gathered
to watch, wary and weary, and the gates stayed firmly shut. For one long moment
your heart thumped in your chest.

But this was never a fight, and the horse was never a trick. It was a gift. All
you had to do was show the weary city that it came from friends.

*What could you do to show them you come in friendship? You carry the conch horn
to sound a warm and welcoming hello across the walls. You could swing open the
horse's door and share the sweet figs. What do you try?*

When you lifted the conch horn and sounded its warm, round note, and then opened
the little door, out spilled figs and flowers and the merry tap of the drum. The
watchful faces on the walls froze, then softened, then broke into astonished
smiles. With a great groan of old timber, the gates of the proud city swung open
at last.

## The Long Quarrel Ends

Down came the city folk from their high walls, and up came the seafarers from the
beach, and they met in the middle of the hill, shy and uncertain at first. Then
one hand offered a fig to another. Someone tapped the little drum. Somebody
laughed, and the laughter spread until the whole hillside rang with it.

Together, side by side, the two camps spread a great feast upon the grass, as
though the long quarrel had been no more than a bad dream now happily forgotten.
Far out on the gentle, glittering water, Thetis the Sea-Mother raised her face
from the waves and smiled to see it. You had done it, and you had done it
together: with your courage, with your loyalty to a friend, and above all with your
imagination. The oldest quarrel of all had ended, not with spears, but
with a single kind surprise. Well done, heroes of the windswept shores of Troy.
