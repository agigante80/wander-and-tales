# The Swan Children's Lullaby

*Read aloud, or let an older child read it themselves. Pause at each question so
the heroes can decide together. There are no wrong answers here, only braver ideas
to try.*

## Before you begin

For as long as anyone in the village could remember, four white swans had sung a
lullaby over the Misty Loch each dusk. Their song lifted the evening mist and
turned the whole sky to rose and gold, and the village settled to sleep beneath it.
But this evening the song faltered. It wavered, lost its way, and forgot its own
tune halfway through. A heavy grey mist crept up off the water and settled over the
loch and the rooftops, and no one could find the sunset behind it.

Old Maeve the Tale-Weaver called the two of you to her side by the warm hearth of
the Roundhouse. "You have heads full of ideas and kind hearts," she said, "and that is
worth more than a loud voice tonight. Will you go down to the loch and help the swan
children remember their lullaby, so the mist lifts again?"

Into your hands she pressed three gifts: the little harp, worn smooth with playing,
a sprig of oak for luck, and a honey oatcake wrapped in a leaf. "Remember," she
said, "here we do not win by being the strongest. We win by being brave, kind,
and full of ideas."

*What is your hero name? Which quality will you carry: Imagination, Courage, or
Kindness?*

## Stop 1: The Grey Mist

The path led you down to the loch, where the mist hung so thick and grey that the
water had all but vanished. Somewhere out in that softness drifted the four swan
children, but the mist had made them quiet and shy, and not a feather showed.

*How will you find them without frightening them away? You might stand quite still
and listen for the smallest ripple. You might call out, softly and kindly, so they
know a friend has come. What do you try?*

When you moved gently and spoke kindly, four pale shapes came gliding out of the
mist towards you: four white swans with long necks and soft, sorrowful eyes. They
had been waiting, it seemed, for someone with a kind face. They drew close and let
you sit beside the water with them.

## Stop 2: Remembering the Lullaby

The swan children longed to sing, but the lullaby had slipped away from them. The
words had come loose and jumbled, and three small verses now drifted in the mist all
out of order. Until the verses went back the right way, the song simply would not
hold together.

A playful shape rustled in a nearby oak: Pook the Puca, the shapeshifting faerie,
who loved a puzzle as much as a prank. He tipped you a wink and offered a small,
sideways hint.

*Can you help them piece the lullaby back together? Listen to each verse in turn.
Think about which one begins the song and which one ends it. Talk it through
together, and trust the picture the words paint.*

When you set the three verses in their proper order, the swan children's eyes shone.
"Yes, that is it," they breathed. "That is exactly how it goes." The shape of the
song came back to them like a half-remembered dream.

## Stop 3: Playing It Together

Now the swan children knew every word, and yet they hesitated. They were shy of
their own voices, and the smallest swan was shyest of all. She opened her beak to
begin, but no sound came, and she tucked her head beneath her wing, afraid to sing
alone.

*This was never a fight. How could you help her find her courage? You carry the
little harp. You might play a slow, gentle tune to lead them in. You might tell the
shyest swan that she is not alone, and sing along beside her so that no one has to
begin by themselves. What do you do?*

When you lifted the little harp and began to play, and your own voices joined the
tune, the shyest swan slowly raised her head. A single sweet note slipped free, then
another, growing braver with each breath. One by one the others joined her, until
all four swan children were singing together, clear and strong and unafraid.

## The Dusk Turns Rose

The lullaby rose over the Misty Loch the way it always had, and the heavy grey mist
began to soften and to thin. Bit by bit it melted away, and in its place the dusk
turned a deep and glowing rose, shot through with gold, until the whole loch held
the sunset like a mirror.

On the green rise above, the Ring of Standing Stones hummed along, low and content,
as it does whenever the swan children sing. The four swans glided in a slow circle,
their song pouring out across the water. And high over the loch, Danu the Kind
looked down on all of it and smiled.

You had done it, and you had done it together: with your courage, with your
kindness, and above all with your bright ideas. Goodnight, heroes of the Emerald Isles.
Sleep well.
