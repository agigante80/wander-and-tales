# Pook's Tangled Paths

*Read aloud, or let an older child read it themselves. Pause at each question so
the heroes can decide together. There are no wrong answers here, only braver ideas
to try.*

## Before you begin

The May fair was only a day away, and the whole village had been baking and
weaving and tuning their fiddles for it. But that morning a worried farmer came
running to the Roundhouse: the winding paths of the Whispering Oak Grove had gone
all tangled, looped over and round with soft ribbons of mist, and not a soul could
find the way through to the fair.

Everyone knew at once who had done it. Pook the Puca, a playful faerie who can
slip from shape to shape as easily as you change your mind, had been at his
games. He meant no harm by it. To Pook, a tangled path is simply the best sort of
riddle, and he had quite forgotten that the village folk would mind.

Old Maeve the Tale-Weaver called the two of you to her side by the warm hearth.
"You have clever heads and kind hearts," she said, "and that is worth more in the
grove than the strongest arm. Will you go and untangle the paths, and win our
Pook over with a friendly word, so he sets them right himself?"

Into your hands she pressed three gifts: a ball of bright red wool, a honey
oatcake still warm from the hearth, and a small sprig of oak leaves for luck.
"Remember," she said, "here we do not win by being the strongest. We win by being
clever, brave, and kind."

*What is your hero name? Which quality will you carry: Cleverness, Courage, or
Kindness?*

## Stop 1: The Tangled Grove

You stepped in under the great oaks, and at once the grove began to play its
trick. The paths looped and twisted and doubled back, and every leafy turning
looked exactly like the last. You walked and walked with great care, and somehow
you always came round again to the very same gnarled old oak you had started from.

A traveller without a plan could wander these paths until the moon came up and
still be no closer to the fair.

*How will you keep from getting lost? You carry a ball of red wool. What might you
do with it? Could you tie one end at the start and let it trail behind you?*

So you knotted the red wool tight around a low branch at the entrance, and let it
unspool gently behind you as you went. Now every step was a step you could undo,
and the looping grove could not trap a hero who carried a thread. Before long you
found the one path that led on, deeper into the whispering wood.

## Stop 2: Pook's Three Doors

The trees drew back and the path opened into a quiet green clearing. There, woven
from living oak branches and hung with soft moss, stood three tall archway doors,
side by side. Only one of them led onward. The other two, you felt sure, would
loop you straight back the way you had come.

With a rustle of leaves, Pook himself popped up: a small furry shape with bright,
mischievous eyes and a grin that could not sit still. He loved nothing better than
a good clue, and he gave you three of them, sing-song and teasing:

*"The left door loves the morning sun, but it sends you back where you begun. The
middle door is mossy and old, and it keeps a fib for every tale it told. The right
door is quiet and small and true, and that is the door that will see you
through."*

*Which door is the true one? Will you read his clues again, slowly, and puzzle them
out together? Pook seems willing to drop a hint if you ask kindly. What do you
try?*

When you chose the right-hand door, it swung wide on a happy creak, and a warm
breeze of grove-smell came through. Pook laughed and turned a somersault clean
over a fern, delighted that you had played his game so well. You stepped through
into the deep heart of the wood.

## Stop 3: Winning Pook Over

Deeper in the grove, where the light fell soft and green, you found Pook again.
But he was not laughing now. He sat hunched on a toadstool with his knees pulled
up to his chin, looking very small, watching his own toes.

He had made the whole grove into a glorious game, and only now, in the quiet, had
it dawned on him that the village folk were sad and that the fair might not
happen. He felt a little bit sorry, and he did not quite know how to say so.

*This was never a fight. What kindness could you show him? You have a honey oatcake
to share. You could sit down beside him on the toadstool. You could tell him there
is room for him at the fair too. What do you do?*

When you sat beside him and broke the honey oatcake in two and shared it, Pook's
bright eyes went soft and warm. "You are not cross with me?" he whispered, hardly
able to believe it. "Then I will set it all right, every last tangle, I promise."

## The Paths Run True

Pook sprang up onto his furry feet and clapped his paws three times. All across
the Whispering Oak Grove, the ribbons of mist came loose and unwound and drifted
away on the breeze, and the winding paths fell straight and true, every one of
them, just as they should be.

And this time Pook did not vanish into the leaves. He skipped along at your side,
leading the way out, prouder of helping than he had ever been of any trick. Down
in the village the folk cheered as the paths opened before them, and the May fair
began in a swirl of ribbons and fiddle music and dancing on the green.

High above the emerald isles, Danu the Kind looked down through the parting mist
and smiled. You had done it, and you had done it together: with your cleverness,
with your courage, and above all with your kindness. Well done, heroes of the
Emerald Isles.
