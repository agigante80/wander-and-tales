# Guide for the Grown-Up

*New here? This is everything you need. Read it once and you will be ready for any
kit in the library. It takes about five minutes.*

## What this is

This is a cooperative story-and-puzzle adventure you play with a child, out loud,
with a printed kit and a few household bits. There is no app and no screen. You read
a little story, your child makes choices, and together you solve gentle puzzles with
simple dice. It is a way to practise imagination, problem-solving, and working
together, and a lovely excuse for unhurried, screen-free time, which researchers
link to how well children settle and thrive. You need no experience at all. If you
can read a bedtime story, you can run this.

## Your three jobs

You are the **Game Master**, which is just three small jobs at once:

- **The narrator.** Read the story parts aloud, warmly. Do a silly voice if you
  fancy it. Pause at the questions and let your child answer.
- **The gentle referee.** When the players try something, you decide how it goes,
  always leaning towards yes. The puzzle pages give you the answers, so you are never
  caught out.
- **The biggest fan.** Cheer every idea, especially the odd ones. Your delight is
  the engine of the whole game.

## The one golden rule: yes, and

Whatever your child suggests, try to say **"yes, and..."** rather than "no". If they
want to befriend the monster, talk to the river, or fly on a borrowed cloud, let
them, and add a little twist that keeps the story moving. "Yes, the vines pull back,
and as they do you spot something glinting underneath." Saying yes keeps a child
leaning in. Saying no, again and again, is the one sure way to lose them.

## Nobody loses, ever

This is the heart of every kit: **a failed roll is never a defeat, only a detour.**
If a dice roll comes up short, do not stop the story. The players can spend an energy
star and try again, combine their two strengths, accept a helpful nudge from a
friendly creature, or simply find another way round. Treat a miss as a funny
surprise, not a failure. No child is ever knocked out, left behind, or made to feel
they have let the team down.

## When they get stuck

Sticking is normal. Reach for these, in order:

- Offer a **friendly hint** through a character ("the cat nudges a loose vine...").
- **Lower the difficulty** one band, or let the helper roll carry it.
- Let them **combine their two strengths** for an automatic success.
- If all else stalls, **just let them succeed.** Momentum matters more than rigour.
  A child who feels clever will play for an hour; a child who feels stuck will wander
  off.

## Keeping a young child captivated

- Do **voices** for the characters; it is half the magic.
- **Hand them the dice** and let them roll. Holding the dice is holding the story.
- Offer **choices** ("do you knock, or do you peek?") rather than open silence.
- Keep scenes **short** and end on a small win.
- **Follow their excitement.** If they love the talking fountain, linger there.
- **Take breaks.** A kit is happy to be played across several sittings.

## Silly or impossible answers

These are a gift, not a problem. If your child says the door opens because "a dragon
eats it", grin and run with it: "the dragon gulps the gate down, burps, and trots
off, and now the way is clear." If an idea truly cannot work, let the world answer
kindly: "the rock is too heavy to lift, but look, there is a gap you could wriggle
through." Never just block them; always offer a door.

## Pacing and length

Aim for fifteen to thirty minutes at a sitting, less for little ones. You may **skip
a puzzle**, shorten the reading, or **stop early** and pick up next time. There is no
clock and no score. The best ending is a child asking to play again.

## A handful of ready phrases

Keep these on the tip of your tongue:

- "Yes, and..."
- "Ooh, what a clever idea. What happens next?"
- "You can absolutely try that. Let us see..."
- "That did not quite work, so what else could we do?"
- "You did that by being clever and kind. Well done."
