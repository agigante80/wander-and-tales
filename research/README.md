# Research

This folder holds two bodies of research, in two subfolders.

- **`evidence-base/`**: the credible evidence behind the idea that a screen-free,
  cooperative, imaginative, critical-thinking game played by a parent and child
  helps build exactly the capacities the next generation will need. It grounds our
  marketing in real research rather than vibes and keeps us honest about what the
  evidence does and does not show.
- **`landscape/`**: a scan of the wider field, the kindred work this project sits
  among. Two parts: digital tools that generate children's stories, and printed or
  purchasable story-adventure kits and games. It studies what each does well, how
  they shape stories for different ages, and what readers and players say, then
  draws out ideas to improve this project. Sample files downloaded for study live in
  `landscape/samples/`, which is gitignored (we do not redistribute others' content).

## The evidence base (`evidence-base/`)

- `01-screens-ai-and-children.md` how mobile/screen use and emerging AI tools
  relate to children's cognitive development, attention, creativity, and
  critical thinking.
- `02-skills-for-an-ai-future.md` what major institutions say the next
  generation most needs to develop now for an uncertain, AI-shaped future.
- `03-play-and-development.md` evidence that imaginative play, board and
  tabletop games, and cooperative parent-child time support those same skills.
  This is the bridge from the research to our game.
- `marketing-paragraph.md` a drafted marketing paragraph in the founder's
  voice, grounded in the sources here, plus the evidence notes behind each
  claim.

## The landscape scan (`landscape/`)

- `digital-story-generators.md` apps and websites that generate or co-create
  children's stories (including AI tools), what they output, how they handle age,
  and what users say.
- `printed-and-purchasable-kits.md` published, printed, or print-and-play
  story-adventure kits and cooperative games for children, their formats by age,
  and what reviewers say.
- `sample-teardowns.md` close reads of real sample PDFs (free and legally
  downloadable) to learn concrete layout and reading-level choices.
- `comparison-and-ideas.md` the synthesis: a comparison and a prioritised set of
  ideas to improve this project.

## Credibility tiers (used throughout)

- **Study (peer-reviewed)**: original empirical work or a meta-analysis in a
  peer-reviewed journal.
- **Institutional report**: a report or framework from an authoritative body
  (WHO, AAP, UNICEF, OECD, World Economic Forum, Common Sense Media, Pew).
- **Journalism / secondary**: reputable plain-language coverage or a commentary
  summarizing primary work.

Each source is also marked **[verified]** when its key claim survived the
research pass's adversarial check (three independent reviewers, two refutes
needed to kill a claim), or **[fetched]** when the source was retrieved and
summarized directly but its specific claims were not put through that adversarial
vote.

## Honest caveats (please read before quoting any of this)

1. **Association, not causation.** Almost all the screen-time findings are
   cross-sectional or correlational. Even the longitudinal toddler study
   disclaims causal inference. Use language like "linked to" or "associated
   with", never "causes".
2. **Effect sizes are small.** The large 2024 meta-analysis effects are modest
   (correlations around -0.04 to -0.16), and one meta-analysis found no
   significant link between total screen time and executive function. The honest
   and more useful story is that **context** (solo passive viewing versus shared
   co-use, and content type) matters more than raw screen time. That actually
   strengthens our parent-child, screen-free case rather than weakening it.
3. **Generative-AI evidence for young children is thin and emerging.** The
   strongest empirical data (Common Sense Media) concern teens aged 13 to 17,
   not young children. The cognitive-harm angle rests partly on university-
   student research. UNICEF and the WEF explicitly say the long-term effects on
   children are largely unknown. Frame AI as authoritative institutional concern
   plus an acknowledged evidence gap, not as settled science.
4. **The play pillar is real but lighter here.** We have solid fetched studies on
   pretend play, board games, and cooperative play, plus the co-use finding, but
   this pillar was less exhaustively verified than the screen-time and
   future-skills pillars. Treat it as well-supported and worth expanding.
5. **One refuted claim, do not use.** A specific coefficient (Munro et al., beta
   = -0.20, p = 0.032) failed verification. Cite the trait-versus-state pattern
   from that study, never the precise number.
6. **Time sensitivity.** The AI sources (2025 to 2026) are in a fast-moving area
   and may date quickly. The screen-time and future-skills literatures are more
   stable.

## The shape of the evidence, in one paragraph

The screen-time-and-child-development literature is well established and points
to a nuanced picture: more solo screen use in early childhood is associated with
modestly lower language, literacy, and cognitive measures, but effects are small
and context dependent, and shared parent-child co-use is positively associated
with cognitive outcomes. Direct evidence on generative AI and young children is
still emerging, with institutions warning that AI is not just another passive
screen and that agreeable, validating ("sycophantic") design may not help a
child still building critical thinking. The future-skills literature is robust:
the World Economic Forum and OECD name analytical and creative thinking,
problem-solving, curiosity, resilience, and social-emotional skills as the
capacities that matter most. And the play literature indicates that imaginative
play, cooperative board games, and attentive shared time support those very
skills. Put together: shared, attentive, screen-free parent-child play supports
the cognitive and future-ready skills institutions prioritize, while we stay
honest that the AI-harm case for young children specifically is still forming.
