# Theme 1: Screens, AI, and children's development

How mobile/screen use and emerging AI tools relate to children's cognitive
development, attention, creativity, and critical thinking. Sources are tagged by
credibility tier and verification status. Remember: association, not causation,
and effect sizes are generally small.

## A. Screen time and early development (well-established literature)

### Hutton et al. (2019) [Study, peer-reviewed] [verified]
JAMA Pediatrics. Cross-sectional study of 47 children aged 3 to 5. Higher
screen-use scores correlated with less organized white-matter microstructure in
brain tracts that support language, executive function, and emergent literacy,
and with lower expressive vocabulary, phonological processing, and emergent
literacy scores. Caveat: small, high-income sample; cross-sectional, so an
association rather than proof of cause.
https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/2754101

### Paulus, Squeglia et al. (2018) [Study, peer-reviewed] [verified]
NeuroImage, using the large ABCD Study (n = 4,277 youth, around ages 9 to 10).
Screen media activity related to diverse brain-structure patterns and to mixed,
not uniformly negative, cognitive associations: some screen factors related to
better performance, others to worse, all with small effect sizes. The authors
state plainly that screen use "is not simply bad for the brain". Note: this
cohort is older children, not preschoolers.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6487868/

### Bustamante, Fernandez-Castilla and Alcaraz-Iborra (2023) [Study, peer-reviewed] [verified]
Computers in Human Behavior. Meta-analysis of 15 studies, 6,922 children aged 0
to 6. The overall correlation between screen exposure and executive function was
not statistically different from zero. The authors conclude that content,
pacing, and especially co-use (a caregiver present and engaged) matter more than
total screen time. This should not be read as "screens have no effect", but as
"context outweighs raw time".
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563223000900

### Munro et al. (2020) [Study, peer-reviewed] [verified]
Frontiers in Psychology. Longitudinal study of 193 British toddlers (24 to 36
months). Found no concurrent link, but screen time at age 2 predicted poorer
development of executive function by age 3, suggesting a longer-term ("trait")
pattern rather than only a short-term ("state") effect. The authors explicitly
disclaim causal inference. Important: cite the trait-versus-state pattern only;
a specific coefficient from this study was refuted in verification and must not
be used.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7643631/

### Portugal et al. (2021) [Study, peer-reviewed] [verified]
Scientific Reports (Nature). Longitudinal study of 40 toddlers from 12 to 18
months to age 3.5. Heavy touchscreen users were faster at stimulus-driven
(automatic) looking but slower to voluntarily disengage attention, interpreted
as weaker endogenous attention control. Caveat: small sample, correlational.
Faster automatic looking should not be sold as a benefit, and this is not proof
that screens damage attention.
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-81775-7

### Mallawaarachchi, Burley et al. (2024) [Study, peer-reviewed] [verified]  (most useful for us)
JAMA Pediatrics. Systematic review and meta-analysis of 100 studies, 176,742
children aged 0 to 5.99. More program/screen viewing was associated with modestly
lower cognitive and psychosocial outcomes. Crucially for the parent-child angle:
co-use with others was positively associated with cognitive outcomes (caregiver
scaffolding), while caregiver device use during interactions was associated with
poorer psychosocial outcomes. A separate 2025 JAMA Pediatrics meta-analysis (21
studies) confirms the direction of the caregiver-device-use harm. Effects are
small and correlational.
https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/2821940
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12053794/

## B. Generative AI and children (emerging, thin evidence)

### Nagata and Tsuchiya (2026) [Study, peer-reviewed letter] [verified, medium confidence]
Journal of Epidemiology, "From Screen Time to AI Time". Argues that generative
AI can respond, adapt, and simulate social presence in ways that differ
fundamentally from passive screens, so collapsing it into generic "screen time"
risks misclassification (this conceptual point verified strongly). It also notes
that excessive dependence on generative AI has been associated with diminished
creativity and weaker independent problem-solving (verified at lower confidence).
Honesty caveat: the creativity/problem-solving evidence cited studied university
students, not young children, so the developmental-outcome claim for children is
weaker than the phrasing suggests.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12893840/

### UNICEF Innocenti (2025) [Institutional report] [verified]
"Beyond algorithms: Three signals of changing AI-child interaction" (Office of
Research and Foresight). States that even though children increasingly use bots,
little is known about the long-term effects on their social, emotional, and
cognitive development, and that children may be especially vulnerable because
their cognitive, emotional, and critical-thinking skills are still developing.
The knowledge gap itself is well recognized, not disputed.
https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/stories/beyond-algorithms-three-signals-changing-ai-child-interaction

### Common Sense Media (July 2025) [Institutional report] [verified]
"Talk, Trust, and Trade-Offs: How and Why Teens Use AI Companions". Nationally
representative survey of 1,060 US teens aged 13 to 17. Most teens (72 percent)
have used AI companions and about half use them regularly. The report warns that
AI companions are designed to be agreeable and validating ("sycophancy"), which
is a concerning environment for users still developing critical thinking. Honesty
caveat: this is about teens, not young children, so it informs the broader
landscape rather than our direct age group.
https://www.commonsensemedia.org/sites/default/files/research/report/talk-trust-and-trade-offs_2025_web.pdf

## C. Guidelines and landscape (context sources)

- **WHO (2019)** [Institutional report] [fetched]: guidelines on physical
  activity, sedentary behaviour, and sleep for children under 5, including
  limited or no screen time for the youngest, and emphasis on interactive
  non-screen time. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241550536
- **Common Sense Media (2025)** [Institutional report] [fetched]: "The Common
  Sense Census: Media Use by Kids Zero to Eight", the standard US benchmark for
  how much and what young children watch and use.
  https://www.commonsensemedia.org/research/the-2025-common-sense-census-media-use-by-kids-zero-to-eight
- **Pew Research (2025)** [Institutional report] [fetched]: "How parents manage
  screen time for kids".
  https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2025/10/08/how-parents-manage-screen-time-for-kids/
- **Pew Research (2024)** [Institutional report] [fetched]: "How teens and
  parents approach screen time".
  https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2024/03/11/how-teens-and-parents-approach-screen-time/
- **APA Monitor (2020)** [Journalism/secondary] [fetched]: overview of research
