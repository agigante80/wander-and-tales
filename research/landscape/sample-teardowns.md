# Sample PDF teardowns: layout and reading-level lessons from the wider field

A hands-on teardown of real, legally free sample PDFs from the wider field of
printable children's stories and family adventure games. The goal is to learn
concrete layout and reading-level/age-format choices that strong printed
children's work makes, so we can sharpen our own Wits & Wonder kits. These are
reference works, not competitors.

**No redistribution.** The PDFs themselves live in `research/landscape/samples/`,
which is gitignored on purpose. Nothing here is republished. Files are downloaded
for private study only. Most are Creative Commons (reusable with attribution); a
couple are free-to-download but all-rights-reserved (study only). Each subsection
records the licence so we never blur the line.

The set was chosen for a clean spread of reading levels (Pratham/StoryWeaver
publish books with explicit Levels 1 to 4, in both English and Spanish) plus two
adult-led family adventure games (procedure and grown-up-guide structure), plus
one publisher how-to that documents a words-per-page leveling taxonomy.

## Per-sample observations

### Pratham/StoryWeaver, English Level 1: "Chunu and Munu Read"

- Source: https://freekidsbooks.org/chunu-and-munu-read-english-level-1/ (mirror of
  the StoryWeaver original, https://storyweaver.org.in). Licence: CC BY 4.0 (reusable
  with attribution).
- A4 landscape, 16 story pages plus credits. The level is signalled in the
  catalogue metadata as "Level 1", aimed at ages 2 to 5.
- Layout is a strict alternation: one page is a full-bleed illustration with no
  text at all, the facing page carries a few short lines. Text-to-illustration
  balance is heavily weighted to art; a "page" of reading is roughly 8 to 15 words.
- Sentences are one short clause each, broken across several lines so the eye gets
  one idea per line: "Chunu, look at this / boy spinning / around." Lots of direct
  speech and repetition ("Chunu, look at..." / "Munu, look at...").
- Lesson: at the earliest level, put text and picture on separate facing surfaces
  and keep one idea per line. The repetition is a feature; it lets a pre-reader
  predict the next line.

### Pratham/StoryWeaver, English: "I Can Make Things!"

- Source: https://data.booksie.org/pratham-books/859-i-can-make-things.pdf (Pratham
  mirror). Licence: CC BY 4.0.
- A4 landscape, 15 pages. A simple first-person "I can make..." structure, the same
  predictable-frame device as the Level 1 book above, which lets an emergent reader
  carry the sentence stem and only decode the changing noun.
- Lesson: a fixed sentence frame with one variable word per page is a reliable
  early-reader scaffold and costs us nothing to adopt in our simple reading level.

### Pratham/StoryWeaver, English Level 3: "Binti Knows Her Mind"

- Source: https://freekidsbooks.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/2408-binti-knows-her-mind-FKB.pdf
  (mirror of StoryWeaver). Licence: CC BY 4.0.
- A4 landscape, 16 story pages. Now full multi-clause narrative sentences appear:
  "Every day, Binti woke up with the energy to move a mountain. Today, she could
  move five." There is a running refrain ("I can do it, Papa, I can") that gives
  rhythm without dropping the reading level.
- Text still sits on its own area with a generous illustration, but the text block
  is larger than at Level 1 (roughly 40 to 70 words per spread).
- Lesson: jumping a level is mostly about sentence length and clause count, not
  vocabulary fireworks. Keep a refrain even as sentences lengthen; it carries the
  younger end of the audience.

### Pratham/StoryWeaver, English Level 4: "Creatures of Old"

- Source: https://freekidsbooks.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/creatures-of-old-Pratham-FKB.pdf
  (mirror of StoryWeaver). Licence: CC BY 4.0.
- A4 landscape, 18 story pages. Rendered pages show dense paragraphs (3 to 6 lines
  each, roughly 60 to 90 words per page) with the illustration shrunk to a small
  spot motif in a corner, and a discreet page number bottom-right ("3/18").
- Craft devices visible: a cliffhanger line set off on its own ("Oh no! Was the
  hunter about to become the prey?"), a single emphasis word in bold caps
  ("GULP!"), and a genre pivot ("This is NOT a story") that turns the opening scene
  into a nonfiction hook about prehistoric animals.
- Lesson: at the oldest level the illustration becomes a spot accent, not the star,
  and white space plus one bold emphasis word does the pacing work. A line set
  alone on the page reads as a beat/cliffhanger. We can use a lone, centred line as
  our "stop and decide" beat.

### Pratham/StoryWeaver, Spanish Level 1: "El Tigre" and "Escuchaste?"

- Sources: https://freekidsbooks.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/Freekidsbooks-stories-el-teegray-cc.pdf
  and https://freekidsbooks.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/FKB-Prathams%C2%BFEscuchaste-Did-You-Hear-Early-nature-book-about-bird-sounds-Spanish.pdf
  (mirrors of StoryWeaver). Licence: CC BY 4.0.
- A4 landscape, 8 story pages each. These are the Spanish editions of the same
  StoryWeaver template: full illustration filling the page with a small text block
  overlaid at the bottom.
- Structure is sound-led and repetitive: an onomatopoeia in bold caps, then a
  simile comparison, then the onomatopoeia again. "COF! COF! COF! / El langur
  suena, como un viejo tosiendo. / COF! COF! COF!" The sound word top and tail give
  a read-aloud adult an obvious cue to perform.
- The Spanish reads as natural peninsular-compatible Spanish at this level (simple
  present, everyday vocabulary); accents and inverted punctuation marks render
  correctly, which is exactly the Unicode-font concern our build already solves.
- Lesson: a sound-effect-in-caps framing each line is a built-in read-aloud
  performance cue and survives translation cleanly. The same page template carries
  English and Spanish unchanged, which validates our locale-neutral-layout approach;
  only the text swaps.

### Amazing Tales (free family RPG quickstart)

- Source: https://amazing-tales.net/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/amazing-tales.pdf
  (publisher site). Licence: free download, all rights reserved (study only, do not
  reuse text or art).
- US Letter, 18 pages. This is an adult-led storytelling game for children "old
  enough to have adventures" (the publisher pitches roughly ages 4 and up).
- Grown-up-facing structure worth copying:
  - It ships two grown-up guides back to back: "An introduction for grown ups" (one
    page, the whole game in a paragraph) and then "A longer guide for grown ups."
    The short version exists so a busy adult can start in two minutes.
  - The procedure is four numbered steps (pick a kind of story, make up a hero,
    make up a story, use the dice) repeated in a loop, stated as a numbered list and
    then expanded with a heading per step.
  - The dice rule is stated as a plain success band: roll the relevant die, "three
    or more they succeed", "one or a two they fail and things get worse". No table
    lookup. This maps directly onto our abstract Easy/Normal/Hard bands and our 1d6
    floor.
  - The adult's prompts to the child are printed in italics as ready-to-say lines:
    "What kind of thing shall our story be about?", "What would you like your hero
    to be called?", "What are they good at?" The grown-up is handed the exact words.
  - A copyright footer repeats on every page; headings use a clear two-level
    hierarchy (numbered step title, then bold sub-labels Name / Appearance / Skills).
- Lesson: give the grown-up both a 2-minute version and a full version, and print
  the adult's read-aloud prompts as italic, ready-to-say lines rather than
  describing them. A no-table success band ("3 or more succeeds, 1 to 2 and it gets
  worse") is the right register for a kid-facing game.

### African Storybook authoring guide (leveling taxonomy)

- Source: https://www.africanstorybook.org/documents/howto/Create_ASb_Storybooks.pdf
  Licence: CC BY 4.0 (African Storybook Initiative).
- A4 portrait, 14 pages. Not a story but the publisher's own how-to, useful because
  it states the leveling rule numerically: five reading levels, each with a hard
  cap on words per page. "First words" allows up to 11 words per page; "Read aloud",
  the most complex, allows up to 150 words per page. The guide also tells authors to
  plan page breaks for 4, 8, or 12 page lengths and to keep one illustration per
  spread at the lower levels.
- Lesson: a leveled reader can be defined by a words-per-page ceiling, not a vibe.
  We could attach an explicit per-page word budget to each (age tier, reading level)
  pair and lint against it, the way we already lint structure.

## Patterns worth stealing

- **Level is signalled three ways at once.** A named level badge in the metadata, a
  words-per-page ceiling, and a layout shift (full-bleed art at Level 1 shrinking to
  a spot illustration at Level 4). The reader, the parent, and the page all agree on
  the difficulty.
- **One idea per line at the bottom levels.** Early-reader text is broken so each
  line is a single clause or phrase; the line break does the parsing for the child.
- **A fixed sentence frame with one variable word** ("I can make...", "Chunu, look
  at...") is the standard emergent-reader scaffold, and a refrain ("I can do it,
  Papa, I can") carries the younger end even as sentences lengthen at higher levels.
- **Illustration density falls predictably with age.** Level 1: full-page art, text
  on the facing page or a small overlaid block. Level 4: dense paragraphs with art
  reduced to a corner motif and white space doing the pacing.
- **Read-aloud cues are baked into the text.** Onomatopoeia in bold caps top-and-tail
  each line; a lone centred line reads as a beat or cliffhanger; an emphasis word in
  bold caps ("GULP!") marks the moment to pause.
- **The grown-up gets a two-minute version and a full version**, and is handed the
  exact words to say as italic ready-to-read prompts.
- **Procedures are stated as plain success bands, not tables.** "3 or more succeeds,
  1 to 2 and it gets worse" is the whole rule.
- **The same page template carries multiple languages unchanged.** Only the text
  swaps; layout, art, and furniture are locale-neutral. Accents and inverted Spanish
  punctuation must render, which means an embedded Unicode font.

## Implications for Wits & Wonder

- Give each (age tier, reading level) a concrete words-per-page budget and consider
  linting prose against it, the way `lint.py` already enforces structure. The
  African Storybook caps (about 11 words/page at the easiest, up to about 150 at the
  read-aloud end) are a sane starting calibration; map our `early`/`young`/`older`
  and `simple`/`rich` onto a similar ladder.
- Let the layout itself shift by level: more full-bleed or large illustration and
  one-idea-per-line at `early`/`simple`, denser text with spot art and tighter
  leading at `older`/`rich`. The builders already take `(world, story, locale,
  reading_level)`, so this is a `theme.py`/`pages.py` decision, not a content one.
- Treat read-aloud cues as a first-class typographic role: a bold-caps emphasis word
  and a lone centred line for the "stop and decide" or peril beat, so the grown-up
  reading aloud can see where to pause. This is a clean fit for our markdown to PDF
  renderer (a dedicated style for an emphasis word and for a standalone beat line).
- For the Guide for the Grown-Up, ship both a two-minute quickstart and the full
  guide, and print the adult's lines to say as italic ready-to-read prompts rather
  than instructions about what to ask.
- Keep dice guidance as a plain success band in kid-facing text (our Easy/Normal/Hard
  over a 1d6 floor already matches the "3 or more succeeds" register); avoid tables.
- The locale-neutral-template approach is validated by the field: the same Pratham
  page template carries English and Spanish with only the text swapped, which is
  exactly our map-template and layout-only model. Keep art and furniture text-free
  and locale-neutral, swap only the prose.
