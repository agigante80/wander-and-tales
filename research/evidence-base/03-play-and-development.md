# Theme 3: How play and cooperative parent-child time build these skills

This is the bridge from the research to our game. The sources below were fetched
and summarized directly. The pillar is well supported but was verified less
exhaustively than themes 1 and 2, and the field carries a real internal debate

## Pretend and imaginative play

### Bredikyte and Brandisauskiene (2023) [Study, peer-reviewed] [fetched]
Frontiers in Psychology. Observational study of 454 children aged 3 to 6 across
seven daycare centers. Found statistically significant correlations between the
quality of pretend play and children's self-regulation, with role performance
("self-position") the strongest predictor. The authors note the long-standing
idea that "in play, the child is always above his average age", and observe that
mature, collaborative role-play develops voluntary behavior and emotional
regulation. They also found only about 30 percent of children reached high-level
play, suggesting academic pressure may be crowding it out. Caveat: correlational,
and based on teacher-administered checklists within a specific theoretical
framework.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10766374/

## Board and tabletop games

### Moya-Higueras et al. (2023) [Study, peer-reviewed] [fetched]
Children (Basel). Randomized controlled trial of 68 children aged 7 to 12 from
disadvantaged backgrounds who played modern board and card games over nine weeks.
Both game groups showed improvements in cognitive flexibility, inhibitory
control, and sustained attention, and the broader multi-skill games did at least
as well as games targeting a single function. The takeaway: ordinary modern board
games (skill based, not pure luck) can support executive-function development,
especially for children with lower starting performance.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10527566/

### Eriksson et al. (2021) [Study, peer-reviewed] [fetched]
Scandinavian Journal of Psychology. Experiment with 65 preschoolers aged 4 to 6
who played cooperative or competitive board games over 6 weeks. Both types
elicited similar cooperation and prosocial behavior, but children enjoyed the
cooperative games significantly more. A useful, honest data point: the clearest
benefit of cooperative games here was enjoyment and engagement (already-prosocial
children may have shown a ceiling effect), which still matters for a game meant to
be played willingly and often together.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8248432/

## Cooperative parent-child time (the strongest verified link we have here)

The single most relevant verified finding lives in theme 1: in the 2024 JAMA
Pediatrics meta-analysis of 176,742 children, **co-use with others (a parent or
sibling engaging alongside the child) was positively associated with cognitive
outcomes**, while a caregiver absorbed in their own device during interaction was
associated with poorer psychosocial outcomes. The mechanism proposed is
caregiver scaffolding: a present adult turns an activity into a back-and-forth
that stretches the child. Our game is built precisely around an attentive adult
and child doing something together, screen-free.
(See `01-screens-ai-and-children.md`, Mallawaarachchi et al. 2024.)

## Honesty note on this pillar

The play literature is genuinely supportive but contains a real internal debate
about how strong the causal evidence is (the Lillard review above is the classic
note of caution). Our defensible position is the careful one: imaginative and
cooperative play is associated with, and plausibly exercises, the skills
institutions say matter, and attentive screen-free parent-child engagement has
the strongest support of all. We do not need to overclaim that a game "makes
children smarter"; the honest, attractive claim is that it is a warm, screen-free
way to practise curiosity, problem-solving, imagination, and teamwork together.
