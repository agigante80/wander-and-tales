# Marketing paragraph (drafts) and the evidence behind them

Drafted in the founder's voice, grounded in the sources in this folder. Both
drafts deliberately use careful, associational language and avoid causal or
"makes kids smarter" claims. Pick one, or mix.

## Short version

> No one can say exactly what the world will look like for our children as AI
> reshapes it. But the people who study it agree on the human skills that will
> matter most: imagination, creative and critical thinking, problem-solving,
> curiosity, and knowing how to work well with someone else. Wits & Wonder is a
> simple way to nurture exactly those, away from a screen. It is a cooperative
> story-and-puzzle adventure you play together with your child, where nobody
> loses and the only way forward is to be clever and kind. And the research keeps
> returning to one thing above all: attentive, screen-free time between a parent
> and child is good for how children grow. This is a lovely excuse for more of it.

## Longer version (leans into the "unknown future" feeling)

> We are raising a generation into a world none of us can fully picture. As AI
> takes over more of the analyzing and the answering, the things that will set
> our kids apart are the ones that stay stubbornly human: imagination, creative
> and critical thinking, curiosity, the instinct to think outside the box, and
> the patience to work something out together with another person. The World
> Economic Forum and the OECD say much the same, naming analytical and creative
> thinking, problem-solving, resilience, and cooperation as the capacities the
> next generation needs most. We cannot know what the AI world will become. But
> we do know that imaginative, cooperative play exercises those very muscles, and
> that the most consistent positive finding in all the screen research is simply
> a parent and child paying attention to each other, away from a screen. Wits &
> Wonder is built for that moment: a gentle, cooperative adventure where you and
> your child solve puzzles with imagination instead of buttons, where nobody
> loses, and where you win by being clever and kind. A simple way to stretch how
> your little one thinks, and a lovely way to spend an afternoon together.

## Evidence notes (which claim rests on what)

- "the skills that will matter most ... imagination, creative and critical
  thinking, problem-solving, curiosity, cooperation": WEF Future of Jobs 2025 and
  OECD Learning Compass 2030. See `02-skills-for-an-ai-future.md`. [verified]
- "the most consistent positive finding ... attentive, screen-free time between a
  parent and child": the co-use result in Mallawaarachchi et al. 2024 (JAMA
  Pediatrics, 176,742 children), where co-use was positively associated with
  cognitive outcomes and distracted caregiver device use with poorer psychosocial
  outcomes. See `01-screens-ai-and-children.md`. [verified] Phrase as
  "associated with", not "causes".
- "imaginative, cooperative play exercises those very muscles": pretend-play and
  board-game studies in `03-play-and-development.md`. [fetched] Supportive but
  the weakest-evidenced pillar; keep the verb soft ("exercises", "practises",
  not "builds" or "boosts").
- "we cannot know what the AI world will become": matches UNICEF and WEF, which
  state the long-term effects of AI on children are largely unknown. See
  `01-screens-ai-and-children.md`, section B. This humility is accurate, not just
  rhetorical.

## What NOT to claim

- Do not say screens or AI "damage", "harm", or "rewire" children's brains. The
  evidence is associational and effect sizes are small.
- Do not cite specific scary statistics about AI and young children. The strong
  AI data is about teens; the young-child evidence is still emerging.
- Do not claim the game "improves IQ", "makes children smarter", or "boosts
  grades". Nothing here supports that, and it is not the point.
- The honest, more attractive promise is the soft one: a warm, screen-free way to
  practise curiosity, imagination, problem-solving, and teamwork together.
