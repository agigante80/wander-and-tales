# Italian (it-IT) quality guide

The single source of truth for writing and reviewing Wander & Tales content in
**Italian (it-IT)**. The goal is prose that sounds like a warm Italian parent
talking to a child, natural and colloquial, never stiff or textbook-literal.

Two things matter most: address the players as "voi" (never the formal "Lei"), and
keep the voice warm and natural rather than grammatically correct but cold.
LanguageTool's Italian is thin (spelling and accents only), so this guide and a
native reviewer carry the grammar and the warmth.

(Full accents are mandatory in all content; this guide uses them so the example
forms can be copied directly.)

## 1. Register: how you address people

- **One child (singular): use "tu".** "Come ti chiami?", "Quale magia
  **scegli**?" Object and possessive: "ti", "tuo/tua".
- **The group of players (plural): use "voi" with its 2nd person plural verbs.**
  "Che cosa **fate**?", "**Leggete** ad alta voce.", "Siete **pronti**?" The
  pronoun is "voi", the object and reflexive is "vi", the possessive is
  "vostro/vostra".
- **The grown-up in the guides: "tu"** (singular), matching the shared Guide
  ("Stampa", "Leggi", "Tieni a portata di mano"). Setup lists that address
  everyone together may use "voi", but pick one and stay consistent inside a file.

**Drop the subject pronoun.** Italian carries the person in the verb ending, so
the explicit "tu" and "voi" sound emphatic or textbook-stiff in neutral prose.
Write "Entrate nel giardino. Cosa fate?" not "Voi entrate...", and "Come ti
chiami?" not "Tu come ti chiami?". Use "tu" or "voi" out loud only for emphasis or
contrast ("Decidete **voi**.").

**The cooperative "noi" is warm and on brand.** Because the grown-up plays along
as a fellow adventurer (not a referee), the inclusive "we" fits beautifully for
shared actions: "**Proviamo** insieme!", "**Apriamo** il cancello", "Cosa
**facciamo** adesso?". A native parent reaches for this naturally. Use it freely
alongside "voi"; it is often the warmest choice.

### Never use the formal "Lei" for the players

The polite "Lei" (with 3rd person singular verbs) is for strangers and offices. In
a family game it reads cold and bureaucratic. Always use "tu" or "voi".

| formal Lei (wrong here) | warm tu / voi (right) |
|---|---|
| Cosa fa? | Cosa fai? / Cosa fate? |
| Guardi qui. | Guarda qui. / Guardate qui. |
| Scelga una carta. | Scegli una carta. / Scegliete una carta. |
| La Sua magia | la tua magia / la vostra magia |

## 2. Warmth: warm and colloquial, not textbook

This is the heart of good Italian here. Write the way a kind grown-up actually
speaks to a child, not the way a grammar book does.

- Reach for warm, everyday words and the little names a parent uses: "coccole",
  "dolcezza", "tenero", "morbido", "gentile", "abbraccio", "tesoro", "amore",
  "piccolo" (native reviewers prefer it to "piccolino"), "campione", "piano piano",
  "che bello!", "dai!", "forza!", "coraggio!", "su!".
- Prefer natural phrasing over stiff constructions. "Dai, proviamo insieme!" not
  "Tentiamo di procedere congiuntamente." "Che bello, ce l'avete fatta!" not
  "Avete completato l'obiettivo con successo."
- Keep sentences light and spoken. Contractions and elisions where natural:
  "c'è", "un'amica", "dell'acqua", "po'" (with the apostrophe, not "pò").
- The euphonic "d" ("ed", "ad") is a matter of ear, not a rule. Native reviewers
  keep it for euphony, to avoid a pile-up of vowels ("ed ora", "ad una", "ad
  esempio"), and that reads naturally. The modern trend trims it before a
  different vowel ("e ora", "a una"), which is also fine. Both work, so go by
  sound; just do not force one or the other.
- It is fine to be cosy and a little playful. The voice is a parent at bedtime,
  not a narrator reading an instruction manual.

## 3. Avoid anglicisms where Italian has a natural word

| meaning | it-IT (use) | avoid |
|---|---|---|
| game | gioco | game |
| team | squadra | team |
| level | livello | level |
| player | giocatore | player |
| score, points | punti, punteggio | score |
| cool, great | che bello, fantastico, forte | cool |

"ok" is the exception: it is fully naturalised in Italian, so it is acceptable.
"va bene" and "d'accordo" mean the same and are a touch warmer, so prefer them in
the cosy voice, but "ok" is not an error to hunt down.

## 4. Spelling and accents

Full accents are mandatory: "è" (is), "perché", "città", "può", "già", "così",
"più". Watch the homographs, because LanguageTool will NOT catch these (both forms
are real words), so they are a judgment-pass job:

- "è" (is) versus "e" (and)
- "dà" (gives) versus "da" (from)
- "sì" (yes) versus "si" (oneself)
- "lì/là" (there) versus "li/la" (them/the)
- "né" (nor) versus "ne" (of it)
- "sé" (oneself) versus "se" (if)
- "è" at the start of a sentence is still "È", never "E'" with an apostrophe.

Also: "po'" (a little) takes an apostrophe, never an accent ("pò" is wrong).

**The three most common Italian spelling errors, get these right:**

- **Acute accent on closed-e words.** "perché" takes the acute accent (é), never
  the grave "perchè" (which does not exist). The same closed-e acute goes on
  "affinché", "poiché", "finché", "benché", "purché", "né", "sé", "ché". Only the
  open-e words take the grave: "è", "cioè", "caffè", "tè".
- **"qual è" has no apostrophe** (never "qual'è"): "quale" is truncated, not
  elided, so nothing is dropped before a vowel to mark.
- **The elision apostrophe is gendered.** Feminine takes it, masculine does not:
  "un'amica" but "un amico", "un'altra" but "un altro", "nessun'altra" but
  "nessun altro".

## 5. Project rules (Wander & Tales)

- **No losing.** Never "sconfitta", "fallimento", or "perdere" as failure. A
  failed try is "un'altra strada" or "un altro modo". Keep the no-lose promise.
- **Warmth.** See section 2; it is the main thing.
- **No dashes.** Never an em dash or en dash, anywhere. Use commas, colons,
  parentheses, or separate sentences. Number ranges are "da 3 a 5", never a dash.
- **Canon names.** Every character, place, creature, and item name must match the
  world `canon/` and the repo lexicon for it-IT. Prose follows canon.
- **Plain words a parent can relay.** Lead with the fun; avoid jargon.

## 6. Soft, associational claims (the Why page and any grown-up copy)

Claims about children are associational, never causal: "è associato a", "un modo
per allenare", not "rende i bambini più intelligenti" or "migliora i voti". The
study about media use is about a parent and child together ("un genitore e il suo
bambino"), not adults in general.

## 7. Reviewer checklist

1. [ ] Players addressed as "voi" (fate, leggete, vostro, vi), one child as "tu",
   never the formal "Lei"?
2. [ ] Voice warm and colloquial, not stiff or textbook-literal?
3. [ ] No anglicisms where Italian has a word (gioco not game, squadra not team)?
4. [ ] Full accents, and the homograph pairs correct (è/e, dà/da, sì/si, lì/li)?
5. [ ] "po'" with an apostrophe, "È" not "E'"?
6. [ ] "perché" with the acute accent (not "perchè"), "qual è" with no apostrophe,
   "un'amica" but "un amico"?
7. [ ] No em or en dashes; ranges as "da 3 a 5"?
8. [ ] No-lose tone: no "sconfitta", "fallimento", "perdere" as failure?
9. [ ] Names match the it-IT canon and lexicon?
10. [ ] Grown-up claims associational, never causal?
