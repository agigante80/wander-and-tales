# Voice and reading levels

This file gives the detail behind the one-breath summary in SKILL.md: how the
two narration levels differ, how Spanish should sound, and worked examples in
both languages. The goal is a voice that a grown-up enjoys reading aloud and a
child leans into.

## The house voice (both levels)

- **Warm and present.** You are sitting beside the child, not lecturing. Address
  the players as "you" (plural where natural).
- **Concrete and sensory.** Name what things look, sound, and feel like. Children
  picture nouns and verbs, not abstractions.
- **Gentle stakes.** Curiosity and care drive the scene, never fear of losing.
- **Invites action.** End beats with a small open question or a choice, so the
  adult can hand the moment to the child ("What do you try first?").
- **Kind humour.** Silliness is welcome; cruelty and sarcasm are not.

## simple (ages 3 to 8)

Covers the `early` and `young` tiers. This is almost always read aloud by the
adult, so it should sound good spoken.

- Short sentences. Often one idea per sentence.
- Common, concrete words. If a richer word appears, the sentence makes its
  meaning obvious.
- Present tense, active voice, lots of verbs.
- Few subordinate clauses; prefer "and" and short full stops over commas stacking
  up.
- Strong sensory hooks: a sound, a smell, a colour, a texture.
- Repetition and rhythm are friends (a repeated phrase per stop, a little rhyme).
- Keep paragraphs to two or three sentences so a reader can look up at the child.

## rich (ages 9 to 12)

Covers the `older` tier. May be read aloud or read by the child.

- Longer, varied sentences; subordinate clauses are fine.
- A wider vocabulary, including a few words worth learning from context.
- More interiority (what a character notices, wonders, or feels) and a little
  more suspense or mystery.
- Same beats and same outcome as the simple version; only the telling matures.
- Still warm, still clear, still no-lose.

Both levels tell the **same story**. Write the simple version and the rich
version of a scene so they could sit side by side and a parent with two children
of different ages could run either from the same map and puzzles.

## Worked example: arriving at the Talking Fountain (gentle peril)

The scene: the players reach a fountain that has gone quiet and sad, and it will
flow again if they cheer it up (a Voice-magic moment in The Sleeping Garden).

**English, simple:**

> You come to a stone fountain. It is not singing. It is not even dripping. The
> water sits still and grey, and the fountain looks a little sad. You lean close.
> "Hello?" you whisper. The fountain sighs. It wants to feel happy again. What
> could you do to cheer it up? Maybe a song. Maybe a joke. Maybe a kind word.

**English, rich:**

> The path opens onto a wide stone fountain, and the first thing you notice is the
> silence. No splash, no trickle, not even a drip. The water lies flat and grey as
> an old coin, and somehow the whole fountain seems to droop, as if it has
> forgotten how to be glad. When you lean in to listen, it lets out a long, low
> sigh. It is not broken. It is only waiting for a reason to sing again. How do
> you coax it back, a song, a joke, a few kind words?

**Spanish, simple:**

> Llegáis a una fuente de piedra. No canta. Ni siquiera gotea. El agua está
> quieta y gris, y la fuente parece un poco triste. Os acercáis. "¿Hola?",
> susurráis. La fuente suspira. Quiere volver a estar contenta. ¿Qué podríais
> hacer para animarla? Quizá una canción. Quizá un chiste. Quizá una palabra
> amable.

**Spanish, rich:**

> El camino se abre ante una ancha fuente de piedra, y lo primero que notáis es el
> silencio. Ni chapoteo, ni murmullo, ni una sola gota. El agua descansa lisa y
> gris como una moneda vieja, y de algún modo la fuente entera parece encogerse,
> como si hubiera olvidado cómo alegrarse. Cuando os inclináis para escuchar,
> deja escapar un suspiro largo y hondo. No está rota. Solo espera un motivo para
> volver a cantar. ¿Cómo la animáis: con una canción, un chiste, unas palabras
> amables?

Notice the beats are identical across all four. The difference is sentence
length, vocabulary, and how much the narration lingers.

## British English (en-GB)

The canonical English is British English. Keep it consistent in spelling,
vocabulary, and idiom:

- **Spelling**: colour, favourite, organise, realise, recognise, analyse, centre,
  metre, grey (not gray), travelled, labelled, maths (not math).
- **Vocabulary and idiom**: autumn (not fall), mum (not mom), garden (not yard),
  torch (not flashlight), biscuit, jumper, "have you got" (not "do you have"),
  "tidy up", "well done". Prefer words a British child hears at home.
- Because narration is read aloud, the strongest tells are vocabulary and idiom,
  not spelling. A line that sounds natural to a British grown-up is the target.

US English is a separate language with its own content files. If you catch
yourself writing an Americanism, fix it here rather than leaving it for the
en-US version, which is authored separately.

## Spanish from Spain (es-ES): register and avoiding translationese

The canonical Spanish is Spanish from Spain (peninsular or European Spanish).
Latin American Spanish is a separate language, authored in its own files, never
blended in here.

- **Address children with "vosotros" for the cooperative pair** (two players
  together), matching the legacy kit ("os lleva", "sois vosotros"). Use "tú" when
  speaking to a single player. Keep it warm and informal; never "usted" for kids.
- **Translate the feeling, not the words.** If the English uses an idiom or a
  rhythm that lands flat literally, find the Spanish that a native child would
  actually enjoy. Faithful is not the same as literal.
- **Use correct accents and punctuation,** including opening marks (¿ ¡).
- **Follow canon Spanish names exactly** (for example "Gato de Niebla" for the
  Mist Cat, "duende solitario" for the lonely sprite). If the canon name reads
  awkwardly in a sentence, fix the canon entry, do not improvise a new name in
  the prose.
- **Match the reading level in Spanish too.** Simple Spanish keeps sentences
  short and words common; rich Spanish may stretch, exactly as in English.
- Read it aloud in Spanish. If it sounds like a translation, it needs another
  pass.
